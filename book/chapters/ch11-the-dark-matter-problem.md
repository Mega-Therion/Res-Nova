# Chapter 11: The Dark Matter Problem

In the 1970s, an astronomer named Vera Rubin made a discovery that would trouble physics for the next fifty years. She was measuring the rotation speeds of galaxies — how fast the stars at the edge of a spiral galaxy orbit its center. According to Newton's law of gravity, stars far from the center should move slowly, the way distant planets orbit the Sun slowly. The gravitational pull of the galaxy's visible mass — its stars, gas, and dust — simply isn't strong enough to keep the outer stars moving fast. They should be drifting away.

But they weren't. Rubin found that the outer stars of every galaxy she measured were moving just as fast as the stars near the center. The rotation curves, instead of declining toward the edges, were flat. Something was holding those stars in their orbits, something that exerted gravitational force but emitted no light. The galaxy had far more gravity than its visible mass could explain.

The response of the physics community was to propose that galaxies are surrounded by vast halos of invisible matter — dark matter. This matter would interact gravitationally but not electromagnetically, which is why we can't see it. It would account for the missing gravitational pull and explain the flat rotation curves. The proposal was elegant, and over the following decades, dark matter became the standard explanation. It was incorporated into the standard cosmological model, where it accounts for about 27% of the universe's energy density. It became, in the minds of most physicists, as real as any particle that had ever been detected.

There was just one problem. Nobody has ever detected dark matter directly. Despite decades of experiments — underground detectors, satellite observations, particle colliders — not a single dark matter particle has been observed. The evidence for dark matter is entirely gravitational. We see its effects on galaxy rotation, on gravitational lensing, on the cosmic microwave background. But we have never seen the thing itself.

This has led a small but persistent group of physicists to ask a different question: What if the problem is not missing matter, but wrong gravity?

### MOND: A Different Hypothesis

In 1983, an Israeli physicist named Mordehai Milgrom proposed a radical alternative. Instead of adding invisible matter, he suggested modifying Newton's law of gravity at very low accelerations. He observed that the discrepancy between predicted and observed rotation speeds always appears at the same acceleration scale — about 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ meters per second squared. Below this threshold, gravity behaves differently than Newton predicted. Above it, Newton's law works perfectly.

Milgrom called his theory MOND — Modified Newtonian Dynamics. It required no dark matter. The flat rotation curves, the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation (a tight correlation between a galaxy's luminosity and its rotation speed), and several other empirical regularities all fell out naturally from a single modification to gravity at low accelerations. The theory was remarkably predictive: given only the visible mass of a galaxy, MOND could predict its rotation curve with a precision that dark matter models, with their free parameters, struggled to match.

But MOND had a problem of its own. It was a phenomenological law — an empirical observation, not a derivation from first principles. Why should gravity modify at exactly this acceleration scale? What determines the value of a₀? And how does MOND fit with general relativity, which had been tested to extraordinary precision in other regimes?

These questions drove a small community of theorists to search for a deeper theory — a theory from which MOND's behavior would emerge naturally, as a consequence of more fundamental principles. This is the search that produced Res Nova.

### The AQUAL Framework

The first step toward a deeper theory was taken by Jacob Bekenstein and Milgrom in 1984, with a formulation called AQUAL — A QUadratic Lagrangian. AQUAL recast MOND as a field theory: instead of a modification of Newton's law, it proposed a modification of the gravitational Lagrangian — the function whose optimization determines the equations of motion.

In AQUAL, the standard Newtonian Poisson equation is replaced by a nonlinear Poisson equation. The key ingredient is an interpolation function, written μ(x), where x is the ratio of the gravitational acceleration to the fundamental scale a₀. This function has a simple property: when x is large (strong gravity), μ approaches 1, and the theory reduces to Newton. When x is small (weak gravity), μ approaches x, and the theory transitions to the MOND regime.

The mathematical challenge is this: what is μ(x)? Several forms have been proposed over the years — the 'simple' form x/√(1+x²), the 'standard' form x/(1+x), and others. Each fits the data reasonably well, but none has been derived from a deeper principle. The interpolation function is a free parameter — or rather, a free function — chosen to fit the observations rather than emerging from theory.

This is where the search for first principles, which has driven this book from Chapter 1, meets one of the most pressing open problems in physics. Can the interpolation function be derived rather than chosen? Can the acceleration scale a₀ be explained rather than measured? Can modified gravity be made covariant — compatible with general relativity and with the speed of gravitational waves, which LIGO has measured to be exactly the speed of light?

These are the questions that Res Nova attempts to answer.
