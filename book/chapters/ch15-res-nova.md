# Chapter 15: Res Nova

In medieval science, there was a category for things that didn't fit. When astronomers observed a new star in the sky — a point of light where none had been before — they called it a res nova, Latin for 'new thing.' The term was used for any phenomenon that couldn't be classified within the existing framework, any observation that resisted explanation by the known categories. A res nova was not a problem to be solved but a door to be opened. It pointed to a gap in understanding, and the gap was where new knowledge would grow.

This book is called Res Nova because every idea in it was, at some point, a new thing. First principles thinking was a res nova when Thales proposed that the world was made of one substance. The mental model was a res nova when Kenneth Craik proposed that the mind simulates reality. Entropy was a res nova when Clausius and Boltzmann showed that the universe has a direction. And the Res Nova research program itself is a res nova — a new approach to the dark matter problem that challenges the assumptions of an entire field.

The dark matter problem is one of the oldest open problems in physics. For fifty years, the standard response has been to add invisible matter. Res Nova asks a different question: What if the problem is not missing matter but wrong gravity? And instead of proposing an answer and hoping it's right, the program builds a framework where the answer can be tested, verified, and honestly assessed.

The theory's interpolating function was first derived from a dual-channel variational identity as μ(x) = x/(1+x). That function was falsified on 2026-09-12 by the solar-system test (Chapter 13). The live function is μ_std(x) = x/√(1+x²), one of the theory's two irreducible inputs: it is structurally motivated and empirically selected, and no covariant argument yet forces it. The acceleration scale measured from 175 SPARC galaxies under μ_std is a₀ = 1.1607 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s² (95% bootstrap interval 0.972–1.295 × 10⁻¹⁰). The cosmological horizon value cH₀/(2π) ≈ 1.042 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s² lies inside that interval, though the connection remains an open problem. The theory is placed within the Skordis–Złośnik covariant framework, which keeps gravitational waves at the speed of light; which branch of that framework it selects is still open. The Lean 4 gate checks every formal module for elaboration with no `sorry`, which certifies the stated mathematics, not the physical assumptions the statements carry.

But the theory is not complete, and the corpus says so explicitly. The horizon identity for a₀ is [O] — open, not proven. The cosmological conjecture Ω_Λ = ln(2) is quarantined to the motivational annex. The JWST high-redshift tests are an interface, not a completed analysis. The SPARC data is not in the repo. The Lean proofs have not been reproduced on a fresh clone. The corpus lists seven open problems and identifies, without romance, what it would take to close them.

This honesty is the deepest contribution of the project. It demonstrates that theoretical physics can be conducted with the same rigor as experimental physics — with controls, with error bars, with pre-registration, with reproducibility requirements, and with a clear distinction between what is known and what is conjectured. The epistemic covenant — [P] for proven, [D] for computed, [C] for cited, [O] for open — is a methodology that could transform how theoretical physics is done, not just for the dark matter problem but for every open question in the field.

The book began with a question — What is everything made of? — and followed the chain of ideas from ancient Miletus to modern Fermilab. The chain runs through first principles thinking, mental models, the physics of everyday decisions, the mathematics of power laws and nonlinear waves, and the frontier of modified gravity and machine-checked proofs. Each link in the chain was, at its time, a res nova — a new thing that didn't fit the existing categories and forced the creation of new ones.

The chain does not end here. The open problems of Res Nova — the horizon identity, the cosmological constant, the high-redshift tests, the fresh-clone reproduction — are the res novae of the future. Each one is a door waiting to be opened, a gap where new knowledge will grow. The pursuit continues, as it has for twenty-six centuries, driven by the same impulse: the refusal to accept 'that's just how it is' and the insistence on asking 'but why?'

Res nova. New thing. The pursuit continues.

---

The motto of the Res Nova research program is: 'AI proposes. Machines verify. Humans audit. Evidence persists.' It is a statement about how knowledge should be built in the age of artificial intelligence — not by trusting machines to be right, but by using machines to check what humans propose, and by preserving the evidence so that future generations can audit what was claimed. It is, in its own way, a first principle: a statement so basic that it cannot be deduced from any other, and from which a new way of doing science can be built.
