# Chapter 13: 171 Galaxies

A theory is only as good as its agreement with data. The function μ(x) = x/(1+x) may be mathematically elegant, and the acceleration scale may be tantalizingly close to the horizon value, but none of that matters if the theory cannot predict what galaxies actually do. The test is empirical, and the data is the SPARC database — Spitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves — a collection of 175 disk galaxies with 3,391 measured kinematic data points, each one a star or gas cloud orbiting the center of its galaxy at a speed that Newton's gravity cannot explain.

The Res Nova team — which is to say, Ryan Yett, working as an independent researcher — ran the theory against the full SPARC database. The process was rigorous: the raw data was cryptographically authenticated (SHA-256 hashes were recorded), the analysis scripts were documented, and the results were recorded in a structured JSON format that could be audited and reproduced. The measurement used 171 galaxies and 3,375 data points, with bootstrap resampling performed over galaxies (not over individual data points, which would have falsely inflated the precision).

The result: a₀ = (1.116 ± 0.128_stat ± 0.097_syst) × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s², with a total uncertainty of 14.4%. This is the acceleration scale at which gravity transitions from Newtonian to modified. It is the number that determines, for every galaxy in the universe, where the rotation curve stops declining and goes flat.

The theory was then tested in a parameter ledger — a systematic comparison between different models, each with a different number of free parameters, fitted to the same data. The results are illuminating. The GOD (Geometrically Ordered Dynamics) model with zero free parameters — using only the horizon-derived a₀ and fixed mass-to-light ratios — achieves a median reduced χ² of 9.20 per galaxy. MOND, with the same zero parameters but using the literature value of a₀, achieves 11.35. The GOD model fits better, but both are poor in absolute terms — a χ² of 9 means the model is not yet capturing all the physics.

When nuisance parameters are allowed — individual galaxy mass-to-light ratios, which account for uncertainties in stellar populations — both models improve dramatically. GOD with shared μ and horizon a₀ achieves χ² = 2.95. MOND with shared μ and fitted a₀ achieves χ² = 2.89. The two models are statistically indistinguishable at this level. The GOD model's advantage is not in fitting — it is in parsimony. The GOD model derives its a₀ from the horizon, while MOND fits it from the data. If the horizon derivation is correct, GOD has zero free parameters where MOND has one.

Against the standard dark matter model — NFW halos with cosmological concentration priors — the comparison is more complex. The dark matter model, with 716 free parameters, achieves χ² = 5.62. It fits worse than either modified gravity model with far fewer parameters, but this comparison is not entirely fair: the dark matter model includes cosmological constraints that the modified gravity models do not. The fair comparison is with NFW halos where concentration is a free parameter, which achieves χ² = 1.92 — better fitting, but with nearly twice as many free parameters as the modified gravity models, and with 97 out of 171 galaxies hitting the boundary of their concentration parameter, which suggests the model is not physically self-consistent for those galaxies.

What this data shows is not that modified gravity is proven and dark matter is wrong. What it shows is that a model with zero free parameters, derived from a variational identity, can compete with models that have hundreds. The parsimony is extraordinary. The GOD model uses one function, one acceleration scale, and no adjustable parameters to predict the rotation curves of 171 galaxies. Dark matter models use 716. The fact that the zero-parameter model is even in the same ballpark is remarkable. The fact that it fits better than the cosmologically-constrained dark matter model is, at minimum, worth taking seriously.

But the data also shows where the theory falls short. A χ² of 2.95 per galaxy is not a perfect fit. The theory is missing something — perhaps the effects of gas, perhaps corrections to the interpolation function, perhaps physics beyond the current model. The corpus is honest about this. It does not claim success where the fit is imperfect. It reports the numbers, classifies their status, and identifies the open problems.

This is what science looks like when it is done with intellectual integrity. Not declarations of victory, but measurements of distance from the goal. Not claims of proof, but honest assessments of what is known and what is not. Not marketing, but evidence.
