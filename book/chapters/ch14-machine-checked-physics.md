# Chapter 14: Machine-Checked Physics

Most physics papers end with a list of references and a note of thanks to the funding agencies. The Res Nova corpus ends with something different: a directory of seventeen Lean 4 modules, each one a machine-checked proof of a mathematical proposition that the theory depends on. This is not a gimmick. It is a revolution in how physics claims are verified.

Lean 4 is a proof assistant — a programming language and a mathematical framework that can verify whether a proof is correct. You write a proof in Lean, and the computer checks every step. If Lean says the proof is valid, it is valid — not because the author is trustworthy, not because the reviewers are competent, but because the logic is mechanically verified. There are no gaps, no hand-waving, no 'it can be shown that.' Every inference is checked by a machine.

The Res Nova corpus contains seventeen Lean modules, verified against Mathlib (a library of mathematical results for Lean), with zero 'sorry' axioms — meaning every proof is complete, with no shortcuts or placeholders. The modules cover the dual-channel τ-tension polynomial equivalence (GODActionKinematics), the trigonometric mapping of μ(x) (MuProjection), the Casimir eigenvalues of so(n) (SOCasimirGenuine), the de Sitter horizon lapse function (DeSitterExtremal), and a spectral gap result called the Yett Paradigm.

These are what the corpus calls 'Leaf Nodes' — formally certified theorems whose mathematical validity is independent of physical ontology. In other words, Lean verifies that the math is correct. Whether the math describes the physical universe is a separate question — one that Lean cannot answer, because that question is about physics, not mathematics.

This distinction is at the heart of the Res Nova epistemic covenant. The covenant classifies every claim into one of four categories: [P] for propositions that are proven — mechanically verified in Lean; [D] for quantities that are computed — directly evaluated from observational data using auditable scripts; [C] for results that are cited — established in peer-reviewed literature; and [O] for claims that are open — theoretical hypotheses lacking closed derivations.

The covenant is not just a labeling scheme. It is a discipline. It forces the researcher to be explicit about what they know and what they don't. It prevents the most common failure mode of theoretical physics: the drift from 'this is a hypothesis' to 'this is established' through repetition and wishful thinking. In the Res Nova corpus, a claim that is [O] today cannot become [P] tomorrow without a Lean proof or a computational verification. The labels are enforced by the structure of the repository, not by the goodwill of the author.

This is a new way of doing science, and it is summarized in the repository's motto: 'AI proposes. Machines verify. Humans audit. Evidence persists.' The idea is that artificial intelligence can generate hypotheses and explore mathematical structures at a scale no human can match. Machines — proof assistants like Lean — can verify the mathematical validity of those structures with absolute certainty. But the judgment of whether the math describes reality — whether the theory is physically correct, not just mathematically sound — remains a human responsibility. And the evidence — the data, the proofs, the computations — persists in a repository that anyone can audit.

The epistemic covenant also includes what the corpus calls 'quarantine rules' — directives that explicitly prevent certain claims from being stated as established results. For example, the cosmological conjecture that the dark energy density equals ln(2) — the Shannon entropy of a single qubit — is quarantined to a 'motivational narrative annex' until a covariant curved-spacetime derivation is produced. The interpolation function μ(x) must be labeled as an 'empirical/algebraic constitutive projection hypothesis,' never as an unconditioned consequence of variational calculus. And the [P] tag — the proof tag — exclusively certifies mathematical inference, never physical ontology.

These quarantines are not admissions of failure. They are signs of intellectual honesty. Most theoretical physics papers make claims that range from proven to speculative without clearly distinguishing between them. The reader is left to guess which results are rigorous and which are hopeful. The Res Nova covenant eliminates this ambiguity. Every claim carries its evidentiary status on its sleeve, and the repository's structure enforces the distinction.

This is perhaps the most important contribution of the Res Nova project, more important than any specific physics result. It demonstrates a methodology for theoretical physics that is transparent, auditable, and honest. A methodology where the boundary between what is known and what is conjectured is not a matter of rhetoric but of record. Where the tools of computer science — version control, automated verification, cryptographic authentication of data — are applied to the oldest question in physics. Where the search for first principles, which began with Thales on the coast of Miletus, is conducted with a rigor that Thales could not have imagined but would have immediately recognized.
