# Chapter 5: Speed, Velocity, and Direction

Part II of this book shifts from how we think to what the physical world can teach us about living in it. Physics is not just a collection of equations and experiments. It is a set of concepts that, when translated into everyday language, provide some of the most powerful mental models available. We begin with the simplest distinction in all of physics — and one of the most consequential in life.

In physics, speed is a scalar. It tells you how fast something is moving, but nothing about where it's going. A car going 60 miles per hour has a speed of 60, whether it's heading north, south, in circles, or off a cliff. Velocity, by contrast, is a vector. It includes both speed and direction. A car going 60 miles per hour due north has a different velocity from one going 60 miles per hour due south, even though their speeds are identical.

This distinction matters in physics because direction changes everything. Two objects with the same speed but different velocities will end up in completely different places. The Earth orbits the Sun at roughly 67,000 miles per hour, but because its velocity is constantly changing direction (curving around the Sun), it stays in a stable orbit rather than flying off into interstellar space. Change the direction, and you change the outcome, even if the speed never varies.

In life, the same principle applies, and most people ignore it. We are obsessed with speed. We want to move fast, get more done, optimize our productivity, pack more into each day. But we rarely ask the more important question: in what direction? You can run at full speed and get nowhere if you're running in the wrong direction. In fact, the faster you run in the wrong direction, the further you get from where you want to be.

This is why velocity, not speed, is the concept that matters. Velocity asks: where are you going? Is the direction correct? Will the path you're on lead to the destination you want? These questions are harder than 'how fast am I going?' because they require you to know what you want, not just how to get it. A person who knows their destination and walks slowly will arrive. A person who doesn't know their destination and sprints will just arrive somewhere they didn't intend to be, tired.

The most common mistake in career planning is optimizing for speed without considering direction. You take the job that pays the most now, without asking whether it leads where you want to go. You learn the skill that's in demand today, without asking whether it will be in demand in ten years. You climb the ladder as fast as possible, without checking whether the ladder is leaning against the right wall.

The physics concept of velocity tells us: before you optimize for speed, make sure your direction is correct. A small course correction now can save years of wasted motion later. A velocity of 1 mile per hour in the right direction beats a speed of 100 miles per hour in the wrong one.

### Leverage: The Fulcrum of the World

Archimedes, the greatest mathematician of the ancient world, made a claim that has echoed through twenty-three centuries: 'Give me a lever long enough and a fulcrum on which to place it, and I shall move the world.'

He was speaking literally about the physics of levers, but the statement has become a metaphor for one of the most important concepts in both physics and life: leverage. Leverage is the ability to produce a disproportionately large output from a small input. It is the principle that a small force, applied at the right point, can move a massive object.

In physics, leverage depends on two things: the length of the lever and the position of the fulcrum. The longer the lever and the closer the fulcrum is to the load, the greater the mechanical advantage. A child with a long enough lever can lift a boulder that a strong man cannot budge with his bare hands. The child's strength hasn't changed. The leverage has.

In life, leverage comes from tools, capital, code, media, and relationships. A person working with their bare hands can dig a small hole. A person with a shovel can dig ten times as much. A person with an excavator can dig a hundred times as much. The person hasn't changed. The tool has. The tool is leverage.

Code is perhaps the most powerful form of leverage ever invented. A piece of software, once written, can run a billion times without additional effort. It can serve a million users simultaneously, process enormous volumes of data, and perform calculations in seconds that would take a human lifetime. The cost of writing the code is fixed; the benefit scales without limit. This is why software has created more wealth in the last thirty years than perhaps any technology in history.

Media — books, films, podcasts, articles — is another form of leverage. A book you write once can be read by millions of people over centuries. A podcast you record in an hour can be downloaded by listeners around the world for years. The effort is fixed; the reach scales. This is why the most successful authors, filmmakers, and creators earn disproportionately: their work has leverage.

Relationships are leverage of a different kind. A single introduction, the right mentor, a trusted partner — these can open doors that decades of individual effort cannot. The leverage of relationships comes from the fact that other people have knowledge, access, and capabilities that you don't, and that a well-placed connection can put those resources at your disposal.

The key insight from the physics of leverage is this: the effort you exert matters less than where you apply it. A small effort at the right leverage point beats a massive effort applied directly. The question is not 'how hard am I working?' but 'am I working at the point of maximum leverage?' Finding that point — the fulcrum where a small push moves the most weight — is one of the most valuable skills a person can develop.
